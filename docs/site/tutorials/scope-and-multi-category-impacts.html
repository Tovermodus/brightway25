Tutorial: scope a study and assess it across several impact categories

Goal-and-scope definition in bw2data terms — functional unit as a demand dict, system boundary as the technosphere graph reachable from it — then running a genuinely mixed set of impact categories (two midpoints, one endpoint/damage indicator) together with bw2calc.MultiLCA.

Everything here uses the real installed bw2data and bw2calc APIs (.venv/lib/python3.11/site-packages/{bw2data,bw2calc}/), verified by running it. See the bw2data module map for the data model and the bw2calc module map for how MultiLCA solves several demands/methods together. This continues from the single-process bw2data example and the contribution-analysis tutorial, and pairs with Example 6's minimal MultiLCA mechanics — here the point is *what the categories mean*, not just how to call the API.

1. Goal & scope, in bw2data terms

"Goal and scope definition" is standard LCA methodology (ISO 14040) for deciding, before any calculation, exactly what question is being asked and where the boundary around the system is drawn. Nothing in bw2data forces you to write this down explicitly — but two things in the data model correspond to it directly, and being deliberate about them is the actual methodological step:

  • The functional unit is the demand dict passed to LCA/ MultiLCA — an amount of a specific product/activity, e.g. {mug.id: 1} means "the impact of producing one ceramic mug", not "one kg of clay" or "the whole factory's annual output". Changing the functional unit changes the question being asked, even if every activity in the database stays the same.
  • The system boundary is implicit, not a separate setting: it is whatever the technosphere graph reachable from the functional unit's activity actually includes, via technosphere-type exchanges. An activity that exists in the database but that nothing in the demand's supply chain draws on is simply outside the study — there is no explicit "boundary" object to inspect, only the graph you built.

Concretely, here's a small system for one product — a ceramic mug — where the boundary choice is made on purpose: cradle-to-gate. The study covers raw-material extraction (clay), a zinc-based glaze, and kiln firing, and stops at "ceramic mug, at factory gate". The use phase (someone washing and using the mug for years) and end-of-life (breakage, disposal) are deliberately out of scope — a cradle-to-grave study would need activities for those too, consuming the mug as their input, and the functional unit would likely change to something time-based (e.g. "one mug used for 1000 washes") rather than just "one mug produced". Neither choice is more correct in the abstract; which one answers the actual question (e.g. "which supplier has the lower manufacturing footprint" vs. "what's the mug's lifetime footprint") determines which is right for a given study. 

import bw2data as bd

bd.projects.set_current("tutorial-scope-project")

# --- biosphere flows ---
biosphere = bd.Database("biosphere")
biosphere.register()
co2 = biosphere.new_node(code="co2", name="carbon dioxide", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
co2.save()
zinc = biosphere.new_node(code="zinc-emission", name="zinc ion", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
zinc.save()

# --- foreground system: cradle-to-gate ceramic mug ---
db = bd.Database("mug_system")
db.register()

clay = db.new_node(code="clay", name="clay extraction", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
clay.save()
clay.new_exchange(input=clay.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
clay.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=0.05, type="biosphere").save()

glaze = db.new_node(code="glaze", name="zinc glaze production", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
glaze.save()
glaze.new_exchange(input=glaze.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
glaze.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=0.3, type="biosphere").save()
glaze.new_exchange(input=zinc.key, amount=0.02, type="biosphere").save()

firing = db.new_node(code="firing", name="kiln firing (electricity)", unit="kilowatt hour", location="GLO")
firing.save()
firing.new_exchange(input=firing.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
firing.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=0.4, type="biosphere").save()

# Cradle-to-gate: production stops here. No use-phase or end-of-life
# activities exist in this database at all -- the boundary is simply
# what the graph below "mug" reaches.
mug = db.new_node(code="mug", name="ceramic mug, at factory gate", unit="unit", location="GLO")
mug.save()
mug.new_exchange(input=mug.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
mug.new_exchange(input=clay.key, amount=0.3, type="technosphere").save()
mug.new_exchange(input=glaze.key, amount=0.05, type="technosphere").save()
mug.new_exchange(input=firing.key, amount=1.2, type="technosphere").save()

print("nodes:", len(db))
for act in db:
    print(" ", act["name"], "-", act["unit"])
nodes: 4
  kiln firing (electricity) - kilowatt hour
  zinc glaze production - kilogram
  clay extraction - kilogram
  ceramic mug, at factory gate - unit

The functional unit for the rest of this tutorial is {mug.id: 1} — "one mug, at the factory gate". Later swapping in {mug.id: 10_000} ("ten thousand mugs, one year's production") or building a separate use-phase/disposal chain and demanding a "mug-lifetime" product instead are both examples of re-scoping the same underlying inventory data.

2. Three genuinely different impact categories

A single climate-change number, by itself, cannot say whether the mug's biggest problem is carbon emissions or something else entirely — that needs more than one impact category, and categories that actually measure different things. Three bw2data.Method objects are registered here, all illustrative/synthetic (not real characterization factors — see the closing note):

  • Climate change (midpoint). kg CO2-eq, characterization factor 1.0 on the CO₂ flow. A midpoint indicator: it stays close to the emission itself (mass of CO₂, weighted by a physical property — global warming potential), not to any particular downstream harm.
  • Ecotoxicity (midpoint, different flow and unit entirely). kg Zn-eq, characterization factor 1.0 on the zinc-ion flow from the glaze. This is not a CO₂-equivalent metric at all — it tracks a completely different substance and a different kind of concern (metal toxicity to ecosystems, not radiative forcing). A study that only reports climate change would miss this dimension entirely, even though the underlying inventory data already has it.
  • Human health damage (endpoint/damage-oriented). Unit DALY (illustrative) ("disability-adjusted life years", the standard unit real endpoint methods like ReCiPe's Human Health category use) with characterization factors on both flows: 1.5e-6 DALY/kg CO₂ and 4.0e-3 DALY/kg zinc. Unlike the two midpoints above, this doesn't answer "how much of substance X was emitted" — it answers "how much does this actually harm people", collapsing different substances and different mechanisms (climate effects on health, metal toxicity) into one comparable damage number. That collapsing is exactly what makes endpoint indicators both useful (one number to compare trade-offs) and more uncertain/contestable (more modeling assumptions baked in) than midpoints.
gwp_key = ("tutorial_method", "climate_change")
gwp = bd.Method(gwp_key)
gwp.register(unit="kg CO2-eq")
gwp.write([(co2.key, 1.0)])

tox_key = ("tutorial_method", "ecotoxicity")
tox = bd.Method(tox_key)
tox.register(unit="kg Zn-eq")
tox.write([(zinc.key, 1.0)])

# Endpoint/damage-oriented: answers "how much human health harm", not
# "how much is emitted" -- illustrative DALY-style factors on both flows.
health_key = ("tutorial_method", "human_health_damage")
health = bd.Method(health_key)
health.register(unit="DALY (illustrative)")
health.write([(co2.key, 1.5e-6), (zinc.key, 4.0e-3)])

3. Run all three together with MultiLCA

bw2calc.MultiLCA solves every (functional unit, impact category) combination over one shared factorized technosphere. It takes demands: dict[str, dict[int, float]] — functional unit names mapped to {node_id: amount}, integer ids only, unlike plain LCA — and a MethodConfig-shaped dict with an impact_categories list. bw2data.compat.get_multilca_data_objs() builds the matching datapackages: 

import bw2calc as bc

func_units = {"1 mug": {mug.id: 1}}
method_config = {"impact_categories": [gwp_key, tox_key, health_key]}
data_objs = bd.get_multilca_data_objs(func_units, method_config)

mlca = bc.MultiLCA(demands=func_units, method_config=method_config, data_objs=data_objs)
mlca.lci()
mlca.lcia()

for (ic, fu), score in sorted(mlca.scores.items()):
    print(f"{fu:8s} {ic[1]:22s} = {score}")
1 mug    climate_change         = 0.5100000278651717
1 mug    ecotoxicity            = 0.000999999992549419
1 mug    human_health_damage    = 4.765000229043943e-06

mlca.scores is keyed (impact_category_tuple, functional_unit_name) → score. Reading the three rows against the inventory (0.51 kg CO₂ and 0.001 kg zinc released per mug, worked from the exchange amounts above) shows the midpoint/endpoint contrast concretely:

  • The two midpoints report in their own native units and can't be compared to each other directly — 0.51 kg CO2-eq and 0.001 kg Zn-eq answer two unrelated questions side by side. That's expected and fine: midpoints are meant to stay legible and close to the physical inventory, not to be summed.
  • The endpoint number, 4.765e-6 DALY, mixes both flows into one score — and because the human-health CF on zinc (4.0e-3) is nearly four orders of magnitude larger than the one on CO₂ (1.5e-6), the tiny 0.001 kg of zinc contributes 0.001 × 4.0e-3 = 4.0e-6 DALY — about 84% of the total damage score — while the much larger 0.51 kg of CO₂ contributes only 0.51 × 1.5e-6 ≈ 7.65e-7 DALY, about 16%. By mass, and by the climate-change midpoint, CO₂ dominates the story; by the endpoint indicator, the small zinc release is what actually matters. That reordering is the whole point of running an endpoint category alongside midpoints rather than instead of them — each highlights a different driver, and a study that stopped at the climate midpoint would have missed where most of the modeled harm is coming from.

A note on real impact assessment data

Every characterization factor in this tutorial (and elsewhere on this site) is illustrative — invented to make the mechanics and the midpoint/endpoint distinction easy to follow, not sourced from any real LCIA method. Real studies use published, peer-reviewed methods with hundreds to thousands of characterization factors across many flows — e.g. ReCiPe, EF (Environmental Footprint), or IMPACT World+ — which ship as importable data, typically pulled in via bw2io (e.g. bundled with an ecoinvent release import, or as a standalone LCIA method import) rather than hand-written with Method.write() as done here. This repo's examples don't have any of those installed, since they require either a licensed ecoinvent/SimaPro dataset or a separate method-specific data package — see the bw2io module map for the actual import machinery that would load them.
