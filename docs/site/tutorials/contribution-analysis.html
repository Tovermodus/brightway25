Tutorial: find contribution hotspots in an LCA result

Once bw2calc has solved an LCA, which process is actually driving the score — and which emission? Walk through bw2analyzer.ContributionAnalysis on a small multi-process inventory to find out.

Everything here uses the real installed bw2analyzer API (.venv/lib/python3.11/site-packages/bw2analyzer/contribution.py), run against a real bw2calc.LCA result. See the bw2analyzer module map and the bw2calc module map for how these two layers fit together, and Examples #3 for the minimal single-process LCA this tutorial builds on.

1. Build a small multi-process inventory

Contribution analysis is only interesting when there's more than one process to compare. Build a tiny supply chain: a widget assembled from steel and plastic, each of which emits CO₂ on its own, plastic also emits a bit of SO₂, and the assembly step adds a little more CO₂ directly: 

import bw2data as bd
import bw2calc as bc

bd.projects.set_current("tutorial-contrib")

biosphere = bd.Database("biosphere")
biosphere.register()
co2 = biosphere.new_node(code="co2", name="carbon dioxide", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
co2.save()
so2 = biosphere.new_node(code="so2", name="sulfur dioxide", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
so2.save()

db = bd.Database("example_db")
db.register()

steel = db.new_node(code="steel", name="steel production", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
steel.save()
steel.new_exchange(input=steel.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
steel.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=1.8, type="biosphere").save()   # 1.8 kg CO2 / kg steel

plastic = db.new_node(code="plastic", name="plastic production", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
plastic.save()
plastic.new_exchange(input=plastic.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
plastic.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=2.5, type="biosphere").save()  # 2.5 kg CO2 / kg plastic
plastic.new_exchange(input=so2.key, amount=0.01, type="biosphere").save() # + a little SO2

widget = db.new_node(code="widget", name="widget assembly", unit="unit", location="GLO")
widget.save()
widget.new_exchange(input=widget.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
widget.new_exchange(input=steel.key, amount=0.5, type="technosphere").save()   # 0.5 kg steel / widget
widget.new_exchange(input=plastic.key, amount=0.2, type="technosphere").save() # 0.2 kg plastic / widget
widget.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=0.1, type="biosphere").save()        # assembly's own CO2

This is the same node/exchange pattern as the create-an-empty-database tutorial, just with three linked process nodes instead of one. widget pulls in steel and plastic as technosphere inputs, so bw2calc will have to resolve all three before there's a score to analyze.

2. Give SO₂ a much higher characterization factor, then run the LCA

To make contribution analysis actually show something interesting, the two biosphere flows need different weights — otherwise "which flow matters most" just tracks raw mass. Register a method where 1 kg SO₂ counts 45× more than 1 kg CO₂, then solve for 10 widgets: 

method_key = ("tutorial_method", "gwp")
method = bd.Method(method_key)
method.register(unit="kg CO2-eq")
method.write([(co2.key, 1.0), (so2.key, 45.0)])

lca = bc.LCA({widget.key: 10}, method_key)   # score for producing 10 widgets
lca.lci()
lca.lcia()
print(lca.score)
15.899999844282865

3. The lowest-level API: top_processes / top_emissions

bw2analyzer.ContributionAnalysis operates on lca.characterized_inventory — the matrix bw2calc builds by multiplying every biosphere flow's amount by its characterization factor, one row per biosphere flow and one column per technosphere activity (see bw2calc's lca_base.py LCABase.lcia() (~line 193), which calls into lca.py LCA.lcia_calculation() (~line 311), setting characterized_inventory = characterization_matrix @ inventory). Summing that matrix along one axis or the other is what top_processes/top_emissions do (contribution.py lines 119-125): 

import bw2analyzer as bwa

ca = bwa.ContributionAnalysis()
print(ca.top_processes(lca.characterized_inventory))
[[8.99999976 0.        ]
 [5.90000007 1.        ]
 [1.00000001 2.        ]]

Each row is [value, matrix column index] — top_processes sums columns (one column = one activity's own direct biosphere flows, scaled by how much of it was produced) and top_emissions sums rows (one row = one biosphere flow, summed across every activity that emits it). Both funnel through ContributionAnalysis.sort_array() (contribution.py lines 6-49), which sorts by abs(value) descending and returns at most limit rows (default 25, or limit_type="percent" to keep everything above a fraction of the total — see step 5). The indices are raw matrix positions, not activity keys — that's what step 4 fixes.

4. Human-readable: annotated_top_processes / annotated_top_emissions

These wrap the same sort with a lookup through lca.dicts.activity.reversed / lca.dicts.biosphere.reversed (the bw2calc index↔database-id mapping, see bw2calc's dictionary_manager.py) and bw2data.get_activity(), so you get real activity objects back instead of matrix indices (contribution.py lines 127-161): 

for score, supply, act in ca.annotated_top_processes(lca):
    print(round(score, 4), round(supply, 4), act["name"])
9.0 5.0 steel production
5.9 2.0 plastic production
1.0 10.0 widget assembly

Each tuple is (lca score, supply, activity): score is that activity's own column-sum of the characterized inventory — the impact caused directly by that activity's own biosphere emissions, not the cumulative impact of everything it consumes upstream (steel and plastic's scores don't include the fraction of the assembly step's emissions, and widget's score of 1.0 only reflects its own direct 0.1 kg CO₂ over 10 widgets — the 9.0 + 5.9 from steel and plastic show up as their own separate rows, not folded into widget's). supply is lca.supply_array[index] — how much of that activity's reference product actually got produced to meet the demand (5 kg steel and 2 kg plastic for 10 widgets at 0.5/0.2 kg each), not an impact number. 

for score, amount, act in ca.annotated_top_emissions(lca):
    print(round(score, 4), round(amount, 4), act["name"])
15.0 15.0 carbon dioxide
0.9 0.02 sulfur dioxide

Here the tuple is (lca score, inventory amount, activity): score is that flow's row-sum of the characterized inventory (total weighted impact from this flow across every activity), and amount is lca.inventory[index, :].sum() — the raw, uncharacterized total mass of that flow (contribution.py line 154). CO₂'s score and amount happen to match here only because its characterization factor is exactly 1.0; SO₂'s don't — 0.02 kg of SO₂ at a factor of 45 scores 0.9, not 0.02. This is the number that tells you a small-mass flow can still dominate the score.

5. Narrow the results: limit and limit_type

Both annotated_top_* methods forward **kwargs straight to sort_array(), so the same two knobs work everywhere: limit_type="number" (default) keeps the top limit rows; limit_type="percent" keeps every row whose abs(value) is at least limit × total (0 < limit <= 1): 

print([act["name"] for _, _, act in ca.annotated_top_processes(lca, limit=1)])
print([act["name"] for _, _, act in ca.annotated_top_processes(lca, limit=0.5, limit_type="percent")])
['steel production']
['steel production']

Pass names=False to skip the get_activity() lookup and get the raw database row id back as the last tuple element instead of an activity object — useful when you just need a fast id to key a dict by, without the extra SQLite lookup per row.

Full example, start to finish

import bw2data as bd
import bw2calc as bc
import bw2analyzer as bwa

bd.projects.set_current("tutorial-contrib")

biosphere = bd.Database("biosphere")
biosphere.register()
co2 = biosphere.new_node(code="co2", name="carbon dioxide", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
co2.save()
so2 = biosphere.new_node(code="so2", name="sulfur dioxide", unit="kilogram", type="emission")
so2.save()

db = bd.Database("example_db")
db.register()

steel = db.new_node(code="steel", name="steel production", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
steel.save()
steel.new_exchange(input=steel.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
steel.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=1.8, type="biosphere").save()

plastic = db.new_node(code="plastic", name="plastic production", unit="kilogram", location="GLO")
plastic.save()
plastic.new_exchange(input=plastic.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
plastic.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=2.5, type="biosphere").save()
plastic.new_exchange(input=so2.key, amount=0.01, type="biosphere").save()

widget = db.new_node(code="widget", name="widget assembly", unit="unit", location="GLO")
widget.save()
widget.new_exchange(input=widget.key, amount=1, type="production").save()
widget.new_exchange(input=steel.key, amount=0.5, type="technosphere").save()
widget.new_exchange(input=plastic.key, amount=0.2, type="technosphere").save()
widget.new_exchange(input=co2.key, amount=0.1, type="biosphere").save()

method_key = ("tutorial_method", "gwp")
method = bd.Method(method_key)
method.register(unit="kg CO2-eq")
method.write([(co2.key, 1.0), (so2.key, 45.0)])

lca = bc.LCA({widget.key: 10}, method_key)
lca.lci()
lca.lcia()
print("score", lca.score)

ca = bwa.ContributionAnalysis()

print("--- annotated_top_processes ---")
for score, supply, act in ca.annotated_top_processes(lca):
    print(round(score, 4), round(supply, 4), act["name"])

print("--- annotated_top_emissions ---")
for score, amount, act in ca.annotated_top_emissions(lca):
    print(round(score, 4), round(amount, 4), act["name"])
score 15.899999844282865
--- annotated_top_processes ---
9.0 5.0 steel production
5.9 2.0 plastic production
1.0 10.0 widget assembly
--- annotated_top_emissions ---
15.0 15.0 carbon dioxide
0.9 0.02 sulfur dioxide

Notes & gotchas

  • These scores are per-activity direct contributions, not cumulative supply-chain totals. annotated_top_processes sums each activity's own column of the characterized inventory. It does not roll steel's and plastic's impact up into widget's — widget's score of 1.0 is only its own 0.1 kg CO₂ per assembly. If you want cumulative upstream contributions attributed to one node, that's a different question — look at bw_graph_tools traversal or bw2analyzer.tagged.traverse_tagged_databases (see the bw2analyzer module map), not ContributionAnalysis.
  • Sorting is by absolute value. sort_array() (contribution.py line 49) sorts by abs(value), so a large negative contribution (e.g. an avoided emission / credit) would rank ahead of a small positive one — check the sign before assuming "top" means "worst".
  • Score vs. supply vs. amount are three different units — mixing them up is the easiest mistake. In annotated_top_processes the second element is supply (physical output quantity); in annotated_top_emissions the second element is amount (uncharacterized flow mass). Neither is the score.
  • A tiny mass can still be the biggest score. SO₂ here is 0.02 kg total — less than 0.2% of the CO₂ mass — but still contributes 0.9 out of 15.9 to the score because its characterization factor is 45× higher. This is exactly the case annotated_top_emissions is for: ranking by weighted impact, not mass.
  • top_processes/top_emissions take a raw matrix, not an LCA object — they're reused by hinton_matrix and d3_treemap in the same file for visualization data structures, so you can point them at other matrices too (e.g. lca.inventory for uncharacterized results).
